Hardwood Floor Water Damage: What Shreveport Homeowners Should Know

It does not take long for water to change a wood floor. Moisture enters each board through its underside and edges, the boards swell and press together, and cupping begins as the edges lift above the middle. A floor in this condition is not necessarily ruined. Solid hardwood that is dried in the first few days often flattens back out. Success depends on a slow, even drying rate that draws water from the flooring and from the subfloor beneath it.

We dry hardwood with floor drying mats. A vacuum unit attached to the sealed mats lifts water out by way of the seams and the wood itself, and dehumidifiers hold the room air at a dry level. Expect it to run a week or more, which is longer than drywall or carpet needs. Sanding has to wait until the wood is fully dry. Sand a cupped floor too soon and it will crown later.

What It Looks Like

  • Blackish or gray discoloration at board joints and fasteners
  • Earthy odor at floor level even though the surface looks clean
  • Board centers humped higher than the edges, called crowning
  • Cupping, where each board's edges rise above its middle
  • Boards that feel soft or spongy underfoot or flex more than before
  • New squeaks or popping sounds when you walk across the wet area
What We Do

How We Handle Hardwood Floor Water Damage

1

Measure the Moisture

We take readings on the boards, the subfloor and unaffected flooring nearby for comparison. From those numbers we learn the depth of the water and if the floor can realistically be saved.

2

Extract Standing Water

We extract any standing water immediately and clear furniture and rugs from the wet section. The faster water stops feeding the seams, the less the boards swell.

3

Set Drying Mats

We tape drying mats to the floor and attach them to a vacuum system, which draws moisture upward through the wood. Dehumidifiers control the room so the wood dries evenly without cracking.

4

Track Progress and Report

We track readings every day or two until boards and subfloor match dry reference areas. Then we tell you whether the floor needs refinishing, a few boards replaced, or nothing.

Hidden Risks

What Hides Behind It

Rusting Fasteners

Nails and staples that stay wet begin to rust and lose holding power. The rust also bleeds into the wood as dark stains that sanding may not remove.

Water That Keeps Moving

Moisture beneath flooring does not stay put. It can reach under cabinets, wall plates and into neighboring rooms, or drip through to the ceiling of the level beneath.

Moisture Under the Boards

Long after the boards seem dry, the subfloor may still be holding water. If it is not dried too, the floor will cup again or grow mold from underneath.

Sanding Too Early

Leveling a damp, cupped floor with a sander takes wood off the lifted edges. Once drying is complete, the middles sit higher and you have a crowned floor.

The Damage Timeline

First hours

Water Goes Under

Seams let the water through, and it fans out in the layer between flooring and subfloor. Underneath, the wood remains wet even after the face has been dried.

24 to 48 hours

Cupping Starts

Swelling sets in and board edges lift. You might notice haze in the topcoat. The bare underside of the boards and the subfloor are where mold can start.

Days 3 through 7

Boards Start Lifting

Pressure grows through the whole floor as the wood expands. Boards crush against each other, fasteners loosen and sections can begin to lift off the subfloor.

Past one week

Permanent Damage

Buckling, black staining and mold under the boards become likely. By now replacement, not drying, is the answer for most of the affected section.
